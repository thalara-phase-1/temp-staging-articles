Very rough draft of my article on industrializing responsibility

## Gist: 

We have industrialized intelligence through AI. Mass produced since intelligence (model) can project into infinite copies and then hosted on mass produced gpus (as a general rule of thumb, handmade < mass produced < infinite projection eg code). 

This is actually very promising since it shows that we can basically mass-produce almost anything, even if it looks elusive or looks like it will remain artisanal forever.

But what is left? 

What is unindustrialized?

Can we industrialize responsibility for example?

This is the last component to ensure a 15 hour work week. by industrializing the responsibility, if we find a way to mass produce responsibility, we can make ourselves less stressed and free of cognitive bandwidth.


## Formalization

To first formulate what exactly we have industrialized and what we have not, we have to first define the question: "what is work"?

Intelligence is only one input.

Although perhaps reductive, we can say that work is something like intelligence + authority + responsibility.

Or adding a few more terms, work is intelligence + authority + responsibility + persistence + resources.

We can mass-produce persistence and intelligence through current agentic workflows, but we are just touching upon mass-producing responsibility.

Responsibility is still an artisan's job.

I asked AI what responsibility means when stripped of moral judgments; it replied "In a work setting, responsibility is something like: There exists an entity such that, if reality deviates from the desired state, that entity notices, decides what to do, acts or escalates, and continues doing so until the obligation is discharged."

"It's maintaining the unresolved obligation: summation of P(something goes wrong) * consequence * my residual responsibility across all items I am currently responsible for".
 (Definition 1)
I think this is a fairly okay definition for now.

## Development

We can ask AI to triage errors, but we are still the ones that own production, and we are still the ones holding the cognitive load of multiple tasks, preventing scaling beyond a certain point.

How many components can we hold at once before dropping something? We can only hold 5-9 things in our mind at a time and that is just the theoretical limit since that assumes that all of our attention is piled on to work.

Referring to Definition 1 earlier, we are failing to automate the residual responsibility. In essence, "someone can have agents write all their code and still feel busy".

## Development 2

Industrialized responsibility: obligations can be transferred to machines rather than merely the tasks belonging to those obligations.

Give the agent not a prompt but an obligation, eg. "Maintain service X at ≥99.95% availability through December 31, subject to these constraints. You have authority over these resources and a $20,000/month budget. Interrupt me only if conditions leave the authorized operating envelope."

E.g. ai's description of this is that "It observes the world, generates its own work, verifies its work, detects when previous work has become invalid, spends resources within delegated authority, recruits other intelligence if necessary, and escalates when its authority is insufficient."

## The Thalara test

Shamelessly using my name for this since I am known to prune tasks taking cognitive load aggressively, the idea is to check to see if we have truly industrialized responsibility.

The question this test asks is: "Can human forget about it"

No periodic checking, no nagging feeling in the back of the mind, etc. Because if we need to check, we are outsourcing execution while still needing supervision.

Safe deletion from the working memory of the human is the criteria

## Current examples

Many startups and products are actually tackling this task of industrializing responsibility from various directions but aren't realizing it. For example, there was a first wave of "proactive agent" startups in 2025 like Poke, and OpenClaw and Meta Muse soon followed in 2026.

Because it's not explicitly said, it's kind of like groping around in the dark; I think having an operational definition about what exactly we are mass producing and what exactly we are selling makes it much more clear why it is selling

## Architecture

Fundamental object is a persistent obligation, dynamically acquiring intelligence, compute, money, tools, agents. We already see this happen but they are not treated as commodity resources because we still have a model-centric approach (start with model, add memory, add tools, add loops, and then add other agents), while it may be even better in an industrial and work context to Work Backwards from the obligation instead (important note: I believe this sort of goal or obligation based thinking is opposite of what should be done in science; we are purely addressing engineering here)


## conclusion

Human workweek will successfully shrink only when human-owned obligations collapse. Agent allows you to do not 2 but 20 projects but can you really remember all 20? If they all work in a system together just monitoring each one in a classical manner is almost intractable and this is a scaling problem and an industrial problem, not something to just grit your teeth through, just like how you don't grit your teeth through manually testing (you just use automated testing), a whole other bajillion examples along this line

Even something intermediate like start-of-week human determines obligations, and then hands-off for most of the week, and then at the end of week, human stays in the loop to inspect, this will already save a lot of cognitive bandwidth. (We can also describe this as declaratively delegating obligations. Actually, declarativeness has taken a large step given the improvements in machine learning over the last two decades, since there is enough expressiveness on the other side to accommodate declarativeness across a large amount of tasks and spaces. We need to expand that to responsibility.) AI's conclusion on this: "It’s a world in which humans primarily choose which states of the world they want to become responsible for creating, and almost everything downstream of that choice can become somebody—or something—else’s problem."
